## What Went Well

1. The epic started at the riskiest boundary. Stock movement persistence, snapshots, typed failures, and migration tests came before mutation UI, which kept later screens from inventing business logic.
2. Atomicity became a real quality gate. Stock In and Stock Out both prove product quantity and movement history commit or roll back together.
3. The UI stayed thin and friendly. Stock In/Out screens parse input, prevent duplicate submits, map typed failures to safe copy, and leave mutation rules inside the repository.
4. History uses snapshots instead of live product joins. That preserved audit readability after product rename or archive, exactly as the architecture required.
5. Review feedback improved safety. Findings around transaction scoping, migration preservation, foreign keys, product filtering, route guard reset, small-screen action tappability, and rollback coverage all became concrete fixes or tests.

Amelia (Developer): "Epic 3 did the scary part properly: it made quantity changes boring, transactional, and auditable before giving users buttons."

Alice (Product Owner): "The user value is direct. Helpers can add or remove stock quickly, but the app still explains what happened afterward."

Dana (QA Engineer): "The important win is no mismatched inventory state. The tests keep proving that quantity and history stay together."

## Challenges And Growth Areas

1. Transaction boundaries needed repeated tightening. Story 3.2 review caught that reading and computing quantity outside the transaction could create stale movement rows or lost updates.
2. Reliability tests had to prove both sides of every failure. It was not enough to assert an error; tests needed to show product quantity, movement history, IDs, and timestamps stayed untouched.
3. Row-action navigation carried prior Epic 2 debt into a higher-risk context. Story 3.7 needed explicit route guard reset and non-vacuous archived-product absence coverage.
4. Presentation tests had to stay focused. Stock In/Out widgets needed to prove validation and friendly failure copy without duplicating repository transaction tests.
5. Tooling friction continued. Flutter/Dart verification still needed Windows temp-copy workflows because direct execution from the WSL UNC path was unreliable.

Charlie (Senior Developer): "The lesson is that mutation-adjacent navigation is not just navigation. Once a row action leads to Stock In or Stock Out, guard behavior becomes part of data safety."

Winston (System Architect): "The feature boundaries held. Products owns product rows and navigation callbacks; Stock owns mutations; History owns read-only audit display. That separation paid for itself."

## Key Insights

1. Build mutation invariants before mutation UI. When the repository contract is strong, screens can stay simple and safer.
2. Audit history needs snapshots, not assumptions. Product names and units must be copied at movement time so History remains readable after product edits or archive.
3. No-side-effect tests should be explicit. Invalid, missing, archived, insufficient, and persistence-failure paths should prove no quantity change, no movement row, and no unnecessary ID/time consumption.
4. Product row actions deserve accessibility and route tests. Small icon actions can be efficient, but only if labels, 48dp targets, and route identity are protected.
5. Reliability hardening stories are valuable even when they make few production changes. Story 3.8 mainly improved confidence and regression coverage rather than feature surface.
